Cosmic Ray Subsystem Report 
On The Voyager-2 Flight Data System Anomaly 
And The Subsequent Data Impact 
 
 
Anomaly 
 
On April 26, 2010 the Voyager Cosmic Ray Subsystem (CRS) team at the Goddard Space Flight Center 
(GSFC) was informed by the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) that the Deep Space Network (DSN) was 
unable to frame-sync the Voyager-2 telemetry starting on April 22 (DOY 112). 
 
An investigation by JPL revealed that the onboard Flight Data System (FDS) computer, which formats 
the data to send back to Earth, suffered a flipped bit in a single memory location. Due to the almost 13 
hour One Way Light Time (OWLT) the investigation of the anomaly and the solution took nearly a 
month to resolve. Thus, there was no Voyager-2 data for the time period from April 22 to May 21 (DOY 
112 – 142). See Appendix A for the detailed emails and web site postings covering the anomaly 
investigation. 
 
 
DOY 143 
 
The first Voyager-2 data following the anomaly was a daily Experimental Data Record (EDR) for DOY 
143. This file, however, would not process using the CRS team’s standard Encyclopedia Generation 
software (ENCGEN). An investigation by Dr. Nand Lal (GSFC) revealed that the EDR contained a time 
discrepancy of about 2 hours and 28 minutes between the Earth Receive Time (ERT) and the Space 
Craft Event Time (SCET) over the expected OWLT difference.  JPL determined that during the anomaly 
the onboard FDS clock did not increment resulting in the timing offset. A time check subroutine in the 
CRS ENCGEN software flagged each record due to this time discrepancy resulting in no data output and 
the failed processing. 
 
To get around this problem Dr. Nand Lal (GSFC) created a specialized version of the production 
software that excluded the time check subroutine. This specialized software version was used to process 
Voyager-2 EDRs from DOY 143 – 175 (May 22 – June 24, 2010). Thus, the final Voyager-2 CRS EDRs 
processed with the specialized, ‘no timecheck’ software are: crsf10143.b, crsf10147.b, 
crsf10153.b, crsf10161.b and crsf10168.b. See Appendix B for the detailed emails 
and notes covering the time discrepancy between the ERT and SCET. 
 
 
DOY 176 and 183 
 
Additional complications arose starting with DOY 176. CRS was unable to process the Voyager-2 daily 
EDR for DOY 176 using the specialized software. The ENCGEN runtime output indicated that the entire 
EDR consisted of  “Overlapping Data” and thus no output was generated.  After an inquiry, JPL 
informed CRS that the most of the data was corrupted due to rain.

A subsequent investigation using EDR’s for DOY 170 – 189 revealed, unexpectedly, that the DOY 176 
EDR would process using the standard production software. The same investigation also revealed that 
significantly more data was processed from DOY 183 EDR with the standard production software than 
with the specialized software. With the specialized ENCGEN most of the DOY 183 records were also 
flagged as “Overlapping Data”.  This investigation was initiated as a data check against the final 
EDR: crsf10175.b.  Because acceptable output was generated for DOY 176 and 183 using the 
standard ENCGEN software this production discrepancy between the two software versions was not 
investigated further. 
 
 
FDS Correction 
 
To correct the FDS time discrepancy on Voyager-2 JPL input a FDS SCLK reset on DOY 179: 
 
2010-179/16:25:57.564 (ERT)  =  2010-179/03:42:57.465 (SCET) 
 
However, JPL informed CRS that the central data system would be unable to put the new correlation file 
in place until the DSS pass was over for DOY 181.  As a result, data for DOY 180 and 181 would still 
have the old clock issue until the new file is on the system starting on DOY 182.

Second FDS Correction 
 
JPL performed a second Voyager 2 FDS SCLK Reset on 2010-216/15:42 ERT.  The purpose of this 
reset was to have the CCS clock and the FDS clock on the Voyager 2 to sync correctly in order to do 
sequences in the future. 
 
 
Daily EDRs for DOY 216 - 217 and the final EDRs CRSF10210.b and CRSF10217.b 
 
Following the second FDS correction the processing of the Voyager-2 daily EDR crs10217.b, failed 
using the standard version of ENCGEN.  The runtime output indicated that all data records were flagged 
as:  “FDSC inconsistent with earth received time” and thus no output was generated.  
An investigation, using the specialized ‘no timecheck’ version of ENCGEN  revealed that part of DOY 
216 and all records from DOY 217 were flagged and excluded. 
 
The same result was found with the Voyager-2 final EDRs 'crsf10210.b' and 'crsf10217.b' 
processed with our standard production software. However, unlike the early problem, both of the final 
EDRs were processable with the 'no timecheck' version of ENCGEN .  The encyclopedia files generated 
included all of the data from the standard programs plus the missing data. No data that should have been 
excluded was detected (Fig. 2). See Appendix D for the detailed emails and notes covering the DOY 
216-217 issues.

Final Explanation From JPL 
 
Whenever the FDS clock is reset, the SCLK and the SCET will be disconnected from the existing time 
trend.  Therefore, you should not use your “timechecks” for your processing for about 2 days until the 
SCLK and SCET sync up. 
  
If we have any clock reset in the future, I’ll make sure to advise you about the event so that you can stop 
your “timechecks” before your processing. 
  
As noted in Appendix D JPL created several additional test EDRs to assist in analyzing the excluded 
data problem. These files were not helpful.

Voyager 2 Anomaly Status Report            28 April 2010 
 
 On Thursday, 22 April 2010, The DSN was unable to frame-sync Voyager 2 telemetry. The downlink 
signal level, symbol signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and the Maximum Likely Convolution Decoder SNR 
were as predicted. The previous pass on Wednesday, 21 April was nominal in all respects and ended as 
predicted. 
 
 Late Thursday, a Two-Way Non-Coherent (TWNC) OFF command was sent to the spacecraft to help 
us determine the best lock frequency for future uplinks. Late Friday, a command was uplinked to 
prevent the operation (NOP) of a MAGROL scheduled for today, 28 April at 9:13. 
Because we were unable to process the telemetry data, there was no way to verify if the NOP command 
was received by the spacecraft. We would be able to tell if the MAGROL occurred by observing the 
downlink AGC. 
 

--- PAGINA PDF 8 ---
 In the meantime, the technical experts were consulted and the team was able to eliminate the ground 
systems, transmitter and RF systems as possible causes. The telemetry is formatted by the Flight Data 
Subsystem (FDS) which points to it as a possible source of the problem. 
 
 The plan going forward was to use the first command opportunity after the planned MAGROL and the 
end of the current command moratorium to send commands to the spacecraft to shift to the 40 bps 
engineering mode (EL40). This will allow the team to check status bits and determine whether the 
onboard sequence is still working. These commands are planned for Friday, 30 April. Subsequently, a 
switch to 1200 bps would be made so that a full FDS memory readout could be accomplished. 
 
 An analysis by Steve Howard of the data received at the station shows that telemetry frames are longer 
than expected by 80 bits with every fourth frame only 60 bits longer. He compared the 26 April data 
with the last data received on 21 April and found that the actual frame time was 48.46 seconds instead of 
the nominal 48.0 seconds (an increase by nearly a half a second per frame, which exactly explains the 
75, on average, extra bits we are seeing in the data. He asked, and it was agreed that the station 
configure a parallel string to try to process the telemetry at 158.5 bps as well as the nominal 160 bps. 
During this morning’s pass, 28 April, the station configured a second Downlink Channel Controller 
(DCC). They still saw the 80/60 extra bits per frame at the backup DCC. The DCC could frame sync on 
those 80 bits longer but not the fourth frames. At any rate, the AMMOS can not currently process these 
data. 
 
 During this morning’s contact it was determined that the MAGROL did not occur. However, there are 
other possible reasons why it might not have occurred. So, to verify that it was a result of the NOP being 
executed and not by some other occurrence, we need to command the spacecraft to EL40. 
 
 Commands to turn TWNC OFF and Switch to EL40 will be radiated on Friday afternoon, 30 April, and 
results received on Saturday afternoon, 1 May. The mode change to EL40 exercise a different part of the 
Flight Data Subsystem telemetry code and removes the Reed Solomon Encoding portion of the 
telemetry.  The next steps will depend on what we learn on Saturday.

Posted on the Voyager website at JPL: 
May 06, 2010 
 
Engineers have shifted NASA's Voyager 2 spacecraft into a mode that transmits only spacecraft health 
and status data while they diagnose an unexpected change in the pattern of returning data. Preliminary 

--- PAGINA PDF 9 ---
engineering data received on May 1 show the spacecraft is basically healthy, and that the source of the 
issue is the flight data system, which is responsible for formatting the data to send back to Earth. The 
change in the data return pattern has prevented mission managers from decoding science data. 
 
The first changes in the return of data packets from Voyager 2, which is near the edge of our solar 
system, appeared on April 22. Mission team members have been working to troubleshoot and resume 
the regular flow of science data. Because of a planned roll maneuver and moratorium on sending 
commands, engineers got their first chance to send commands to the spacecraft on April 30. It takes 
nearly 13 hours for signals to reach the spacecraft and nearly 13 hours for signals to come down to 
NASA's Deep Space Network on Earth. 
 
 
Posted on the Voyager website at JPL: 
Updated May 17, 2010 at 5:00 p.m. PDT 
 
One flip of a bit in the memory of an onboard computer appears to have caused the change in the science 
data pattern returning from Voyager 2, engineers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory said Monday, 
May 17. A value in a single memory location was changed from a 0 to a 1. 
 
On May 12, engineers received a full memory readout from the flight data system computer, which 
formats the data to send back to Earth. They isolated the one bit in the memory that had changed, and 
they recreated the effect on a computer at JPL. They found the effect agrees with data coming down 
from the spacecraft. They are planning to reset the bit to its normal state on Wednesday, May 19. 
 
 
Posted on the Voyager website at JPL: 
Updated May 20, 2010 at 6:00 p.m. PDT 
 
Engineers have successfully corrected the memory on NASA's Voyager 2 spacecraft by resetting a 
computer bit that had flipped. Reset commands were beamed up to the spacecraft yesterday, Wed., May 
19, and engineering data received today confirm that the reset was successful. The Voyager team will 
continue monitoring the engineering data, and if the bit remains reset, commands to switch to the 
science data mode will be beamed up to Voyager 2 on Sat., May 22. Receipt of science data would then 
resume on Sun., May 23.

Engineers successfully reset a computer onboard Voyager 2 that caused an unexpected data pattern shift, 
and the spacecraft resumed sending properly formatted science data back to Earth on Sunday, May 23. 
Mission managers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Pasadena, Calif., had been operating the 
spacecraft in engineering mode since May 6. They took this action as they traced the source of the

back to Earth.

instruments onboard the spacecraft are processing data correctly.
